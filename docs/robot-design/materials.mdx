# Materials

While many materials can be used successfully in beetleweight combat robots, there are several that are especially popular due to their balance of accessibility, cost, and durability. Bear in mind that this is not a comprehensive list of every material that could be useful in a beetle - just the most common ones.

## Metals

![AR steel sheet](/img/wiki/materials/ar-steel-sheet.jpg)

*AR steel wear plate - pre-hardened and easily cut into 2D weapon shapes.*

### AR Steel Plate
A hardened steel wear plate. It comes in a few common varieties ranging from **AR400** to **AR600** with the number representing the material's approximate Brinell hardness. Because it comes in pre-hardened sheets, it can easily be cut into 2D shapes with no post-processing required.

- **Pros:** Very hard and tough, making it an excellent material for weapons and forks - in fact, it is the most common material for these things. It is also relatively cheap compared to other alloy steels making it an economical choice.
- **Cons:** Can only be cut into flat 2D profiles. AR steel can technically be welded, but this is difficult and not recommended. AR500 and below can be bent but a very powerful bending brake is required. The main processing method for AR steel, laser cutting, anneals the cut edges due to the heat, causing them to lose hardness. Waterjet cutting avoids this issue, but as of the time of writing there are no popular large-scale suppliers that offer waterjet AR steel.

### 6Al-4V Titanium
Also known as **Grade 5** (USA) or **TC4** (China) titanium, is another popular material in beetleweights. Titanium is most commonly used for anti-horizontal wedges, as it is lighter than steel and strong enough to take hits. It can also be bent relatively easily with a torch and hand tools – a brief guide on how to do this can be found in [Appendix II](/appendices/bending-titanium).

- **Pros:** Great for anti-horizontal wedges, stiffening plates, weapon shafts, spiked "cleat" wheels, and top armor against overhead attackers.
- **Cons:** Lower hardness and higher cost makes it generally a worse choice than AR steel for weapons and forks. CNC machining into 3D shapes is possible but very expensive. More commonly, it is laser cut into 2D profiles and bent as necessary.
- **Note:** Make sure you get the right alloy! Grade 2 "commercially pure" titanium is common and cheaper than 6Al-4V, but much weaker and unsuitable for combat robots.

![Titanium wedge](/img/wiki/materials/titanium-wedge.png)

*A titanium anti-horizontal wedge.*

### Aluminum
Is less commonly used than titanium and AR steel, but still serves an important purpose in many robots. There are many grades of aluminum available, but the most commonly used are **6061** and **7075**, where 7075 is a bit stronger but more brittle than 6061.

2D laser-cut aluminum is very cheap and used for stiffening and mounting plates, as well as top and bottom panels. It is also used in components where both high strength and 3D geometry are needed, such as custom pulleys, motor mounts, drive axles, and such as it is one of the cheapest and easiest materials to CNC machine.

### Hardenable Alloy Steels
Such as **4140** and **4340**, as well as tool steels such as **S7** and **H13**, are popular materials for weapons that require 3D geometry such as drums and beaters. They are CNC machined into a 3D shape, and then hardened to achieve maximum strength.

Hardening these steels is a delicate balance between hardness and toughness: too soft, and the weapon will wear and deform quickly - too hard, and the weapon may shatter on hard impacts. 4140 and 4340 are relatively forgiving in terms of hardness ranges, while tool steels can hit higher hardnesses but require more precise adjustment depending on the particular material and weapon geometry.

## Plastics

### UHMW
**Ultra-High Molecular Weight Polyethylene (UHMW)** is one of the most common materials in beetleweight combat robots. It is an extremely tough plastic that is also rigid enough for frame components.

- **Pros:** Inexpensive, extremely lightweight, and takes hits very well, gouging and deforming instead of shattering or cracking like other plastics. Commonly used as structural chassis components, weapon bulkheads, and armor.
- **Manufacturing:** Generally cut on a CNC router, which allows for 2D profiles as well as 2.5D features like partial-depth pockets. It can also be fully 3D machined into things like a full unibody chassis.
- **HDPE** is a more common and cheaper alternative to UHMW, but it is more brittle and not recommended when UHMW is an option. However, common plastic cutting boards are made of HDPE and can serve as a decent option for bots on a tight budget.

### 3D-Printed TPU
**Thermoplastic Polyurethane (TPU)** is arguably the single most versatile and popular material in beetleweights. You'll be hard pressed to find a single beetle that doesn't have some TPU in it.

- **Pros:** Because of its flexibility, it is extremely durable and difficult to break. Additionally, because it can be 3D printed, it can be manufactured very cheaply and complex shapes are possible.
- **Uses:** TPU can be used for almost anything - armor, pulleys, wheels, fork and wedge mounts, internal mounts and dividers, arms for lifters and overhead attackers, forks and wedgelets, even entire chassis.
- **Hardness:** TPU comes in several hardnesses - **95A** is most popular for combat robots as it is commonly available and flexible enough to take hits. Harder TPUs can work but risk cracking when hit.

![TPU part](/img/wiki/materials/tpu-part.png)

*3D-printed TPU parts - flexible, extremely durable, and cheap to manufacture.*

### 3D-Printed Nylon
Another popular material in combat robotics. While too brittle to be used as armor when compared to TPU, its higher rigidity, better temperature and wear resistance, and low friction make it a great choice for mechanical parts like pulleys, gears, and spacers, as well as parts that need to be rigid such as motor mounts.

- **Cons:** It is difficult to print as it absorbs water very readily and warps quite badly.
- **Filled variants:** Nylon also comes in carbon-fiber and glass-fiber reinforced variants that increase its rigidity and reduce warping at the cost of increased brittleness.

*3D-printed nylon - rigid and low-friction, ideal for mechanical parts.*

### 3D-Printed PLA+
Not a material that one would expect to be common in combat robotics, but here's the thing - while regular PLA is too weak to be viable in beetleweight robots, modern PLA+ blends such as **Overture Super PLA+** and **Polymaker Polymax PLA** vastly increased toughness, surpassing even nylon at times.

- **Pros:** Inexpensive and very easy to print. Useful for internal mounts and dividers, and can even be successfully used as a chassis material.
- **Cons:** Low temperature resistance - be careful as hot motors and electronics can sometimes cause it to deform.

## Composites

### Carbon Fiber
Boasts extremely high strength and rigidity at a low weight. In beetleweight robots, it is very commonly used for rigid top and bottom panels, as well as stiffening and mounting plates that need to be strong and rigid.

- **Cons:** Brittle and has poor abrasion resistance, making it unsuitable for parts that could take direct hits. Carbon fiber is conductive, so it is not recommended to have it in direct contact with electronic components. The dust it produces when cut is highly carcinogenic.
- **Manufacturing:** Most often cut into 2D or 2.5D shapes on a CNC router, but it can also be waterjetted.

### Garolite
Also known as **G10**, **FR4**, or fiberglass composite, is a popular alternative to carbon fiber. It is very similar, but is made with glass fibers instead of carbon fibers.

- Compared to CF, it is weaker, less rigid, and heavier, but is cheaper and non-conductive.
- Additionally, when a CF part is hit, often the epoxy fails before the fibers, causing delamination to propagate along the part. Because glass fibers are weaker than carbon fibers, they tend to fail more quickly causing damage to be localized - one of its main benefits over CF.

### Tegris
A brand-name of **self-reinforcing polypropylene (SRPP)** composite that has been increasing in popularity recently.

- At just over half the weight of carbon fiber, it is shockingly lightweight, and is much more rigid than other plastic materials.
- Not as good of a structural material as carbon fiber or Garolite due to its lower rigidity and strength, though it still can be used as top and bottom panels where weight is of major concern.
- In thicker sheets, it makes a great chassis material, and it takes hits much better than CF or Garolite allowing it to be used as armor.
- Can be easily cut with a common 40-60W CO2 laser found at many shops and makerspaces, making it very accessible to work with. It can also be bent easily, and thin sheets can be cut by hand with a good pair of scissors or aviation snips.

## Material Selection

Of course, different materials are ideal for different purposes. It's easy to simply view materials as weak/strong or rigid/flexible, but material properties in reality are a lot more complex than that. In a combat environment especially, it is crucial to consider how materials behave in impact scenarios, as well as how they fail.

For example, consider armor for your robot's wheels. You may be tempted to use a metal such as steel or titanium because it is "stronger", but this is generally a poor choice as metals tend to deform permanently under impact loads – when hit, they are likely to bend into your wheels and jam them up. On the other hand, a flexible plastic like TPU or UHMW is a much better choice as these materials are much better able to return to their original shape after sustaining a hit.

Similarly, aluminum is often a poor choice for top and bottom plates as it tends to bend and peel when hit which can end up high-centering your robot. On the other hand, materials like carbon fiber are more likely to splinter or break off in chunks reducing the chances of high-centering.

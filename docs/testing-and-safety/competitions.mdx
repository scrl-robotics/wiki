# Tips for Competitions

Now that your robot is all tested and ready to go, it's time to finally attend your first event! Attending an event for the first time can be quite stressful if you're not adequately prepared, so this section aims to provide some tips on how to ensure you have a fun and comfortable experience.

## Recommended Preparation

There are a few things that are recommended to do before attending a competition to make sure things go as smoothly as possible before and between fights. While everything may seem good at home, the pressure of competition can make a lot of things more difficult so it's a good idea to prepare adequately beforehand.

### Practice assembling and disassembling your robot quickly
Most events only guarantee a minimum of **20-30 or so minutes between fights**, and it's very rare to get more than this during final rounds. It's very important to be able to quickly take apart and repair your robot under pressure and in tight timelines.

Practice swapping out important parts like weapon assemblies, drive motors, armor pieces, etc. If you're finding that your robot is difficult to work on, it might be worth making some design changes to make things easier to disassemble and repair.

### Make sure your robot is fully assembled and safety-tested beforehand
All events require on-site safety testing where you must demonstrate that your robot is in weight, able to move in a controlled manner, and failsafes properly. Don't wait until the competition to do this! Always make sure you have a fully assembled and safety-tested robot ready to go as soon as you arrive.

Even if you don't have a test box at home, safety-testing can be done using the drive system with the weapon lock in place. Make sure your robot drives properly, and that all systems are working properly (weapon systems can be safely tested by disconnecting the motor from the weapon).

### Make sure your spares are as assembled as possible
Spares are discussed in the next section, but if you're bringing spares make sure that they are all **ready to be dropped into the robot**. For example:
- Fully assemble any spare sub-assemblies or armor packages
- Make sure spare electronics are properly programmed and have connectors attached
- Pack screws and other hardware together with their associated parts

This makes it much easier to swap out damaged components on the fly.

### Keep your parts organized
There's nothing worse than needing to make repairs with the EO breathing down your neck for your next fight and being unable to find a spare or tool that you need. Make sure that everything you pack is properly organized and remains so throughout the event.

Rolling toolcases like Packout sets or their cheaper knockoffs are great, but a simple suitcase with some parts organizers works as well. 3D printer filament boxes or McMaster-Carr boxes with Ziploc bags are also a great budget option.

### Arrive as early as possible
Sometimes weird issues show up at the competition that don't at home. Maybe the event's scale is calibrated differently than yours and now you're a few grams overweight, or maybe the arena's floor is different than the one in your test box and you need to re-tune your drive. Or, sometimes things just decide to not work for no reason at all.

Arriving early allows you to iron out any issues before your first fight.

### Bring sufficient food and water
This one may sound out of place, but it's very easy to forget to eat and drink in the heat of a competition! Additionally, some events are hosted at large conventions or expos where food and drink may be difficult to find and expensive.

It's always a good idea to pack a bottle of Gatorade and an extra breakfast sandwich when competing in case there's no time to grab lunch.

## Spares

One of the most common questions asked by beginners before a competition is "**how many spares should I bring?**" This question depends heavily on your style of robot and budget, but there are some basic guidelines that can help you determine how much money you want to spend on spares.

Here are some types of parts that are usually worth bringing spares for:

### External Parts

| Part | Recommendation |
|------|---------------|
| **3D-printed parts** | These are very cheap to manufacture so it's always worth bringing a lot of spares of printed parts. TPU armor packages can get torn up pretty quickly so bring lots of spares: at least 2 spare sets of TPU armor. |
| **Forks** | These are crucial to the functioning of many archetypes of robots but can get worn down quickly especially if hit. Laser-cut metal forks are quite inexpensive when purchased in large quantities so it's usually worth bringing a few extra sets. |
| **Weapon blades** | These can wear down very quickly and become imbalanced or lose their tooth sharpness. 2D laser-cut weapons are also quite inexpensive in large quantities. |
| **Wheels** | It's always a good idea to bring as many wheels as you can. They can be easily damaged if hit directly, or simply wear down. A robot cannot compete without wheels, and if you're making custom wheels the marginal effort to make a few more is quite small so bring spares! |

### Internal Parts

| Part | Recommendation |
|------|---------------|
| **Drive motors** | These are the most commonly damaged electronic components as they can break quite easily if the wheel gets hit directly. Unfortunately, drive motors are often quite expensive making stockpiling them unrealistic for most. However, bringing at least one spare drive motor is recommended. |
| **Batteries** | It's always a good idea to bring at least two batteries, so you can cycle through them while charging. It's also not uncommon to accidentally over-discharge a battery during a fight and render it unsafe to continue using so having a spare is important. |
| **Receivers** | These are cheap and while not often damaged, cheaper receivers can be "finicky" at times so having another one you can swap to if your current one is acting up is helpful. |

### Spare Complete Robots

It's becoming more and more common for builders to bring **fully-assembled spare copies of their robots** to ensure they are able to fight if their bot is totaled during a fight and repair windows are too short. While this certainly makes competing much easier, it is not recommended for a first-time bot as it can be rather expensive, and you will likely make many design changes after your first few competitions that can write off a lot of expensive parts.

Wait until you're confident in your robot's design and won't be changing it significantly in the foreseeable future before considering assembling a second copy.

## Tools

Bringing your entire toolkit is often unrealistic, so here are some important tools that are recommended to bring to competitions:

| Tool | Why |
|------|-----|
| **Electronics screwdriver set** | These have many interchangeable bits that can be used for the vast majority of hardware used in beetleweights. The iFixit ones are a popular choice. |
| **Locking pliers/vice grips** | These are extremely versatile tools that can be used to clamp parts for cutting, bend parts, grab onto damaged screws or nuts, or even lock weapons. |
| **Rotary tool (AKA Dremel)** | Another incredibly versatile tool that can be used for a variety of purposes. Need to cut off damaged parts? Resharpen worn-down weapon blades or forks? Cut new holes or slots to repurpose spare parts or fresh sheets of material? A rotary tool can do all that for you. |
| **Soldering kit** | It's important to be able to repair your electronic system at competition. A soldering iron (the Pinecil is an excellent portable option), leaded solder, and cleaning sponge in addition to some spare wire, heatshrink, electrical tape, and connectors may not be used often but will absolutely save you if any of your electronics die. |
| **Wire cutters or flush cutters** | These are very useful for cutting and trimming plastic or composite parts, and are more convenient than using a rotary tool. |
| **High-strength tape** | Always bring a roll of Gorilla tape as well as a roll of fiber-reinforced strapping tape to competitions. Never know when you'll need to strap two things together in a pinch. |
| **Drill** | A drill and basic set of drill bits can be a life-saver if one of your screw holes becomes unusably damaged and you need to create a new one elsewhere to hold your robot together. |

Additionally, if you need any specific tool to assemble your robot, obviously bring that. How many tools you bring depends on how much weight you want to carry. However, do keep in mind that most builders are willing to **lend tools to fellow competitors**, so it's not the end of the world if you miss something.

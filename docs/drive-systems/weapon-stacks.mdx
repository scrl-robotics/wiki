# Spinner Weapon Systems

Now that you (hopefully) have designed a powerful and durable spinning weapon, the next step is to figure out how to mount it to your robot. The vast majority of weapons are mounted on a **deadshaft**, with bearings pressed into the weapon. Power is transferred from the motor to the weapon via belts or gears, which isolates the motor from the shock loads experienced by the weapon.

## Bearings

The majority of robots will use either **ball bearings** or **needle-roller bearings** in spinning weapons.

![Bearing types](/img/wiki/weapon-stacks/bearing-types.png)

*Ball bearings (left) and needle-roller bearings (right) are the two most common weapon bearing types.*

### Ball Bearings
- **Pros:** Lower friction, lower slop, and having an inner race that can be used to take up axial loads on the bearing.
- **Cons:** Due to the balls' small contact patch with the inner and outer races, ball bearings are more fragile than other options. Additionally, while the inner race can be used alongside spacers to constrain the bearing axially, excessively high axial loads, such as forces from an off-plane hit, can cause them to explode due to only half the race being load-bearing under axial load.

Ball bearings are popular in larger diameter weapons where low slop is crucial, and many builders will choose to use **angular-contact ball bearings** which can take axial loads much more effectively as the entire race is load-bearing, but only in one direction.

![Ball bearing](/img/wiki/weapon-stacks/ball-bearing.png)

*Ball bearings offer lower friction, but are more fragile under axial load than needle-roller bearings.*

### Needle-Roller Bearings
- Use thin cylindrical rollers instead of balls to reduce friction.
- Have **no inner race**: the rollers roll directly on the shaft.
- **Pros:** Because the rollers are quite wide and have a large contact patch with the shaft and outer race, they are far more durable than ball bearings. They are also much thinner, making them easier to fit into small-diameter weapons.
- **Cons:** Less precise than ball bearings and have a tendency to wobble a little bit on the shaft, so it is important to use at least two bearings spaced far apart to reduce this wobble. Additionally, they have no inner race to take up axial loads, so **thrust washers are necessary**.

Needle-roller bearings are the best choice for vertical spinners due to their smaller diameter. Because the rollers roll directly on the shaft rather than on an inner race, it is recommended to use a **polished hardened steel shaft** with needle-rollers. Shafts made of softer material such as titanium or alloy steel can work, but will become indented by the rollers over time making them disposable. **Do not use aluminum shafts with needle-roller bearings.**

![Needle-roller bearing](/img/wiki/weapon-stacks/needle-roller-bearing.png)

*Needle-roller bearings roll directly on the shaft and need thrust washers to take axial loads.*

:::note On bearing quality
It's generally accepted in combat robotics that more expensive, higher quality (ABEC) bearings are not necessary, as the additional precision of these bearings is destroyed very quickly with weapon impacts. Any cheap bearings will work perfectly fine. **Uxcell** brand ones off Amazon are a very popular choice, or you can get bearings for as little as $0.50 apiece off Aliexpress.
:::

## Shafts

There are a few different off the shelf components that can work well as a weapon deadshaft.

### Shoulder Bolts
The cheapest and easiest is a **shoulder bolt**, which is essentially a bolt with a precision-ground shoulder that makes up most of its length and can function as a shaft. They can be found in various sizes on **McMaster-Carr**, and metric versions can be found for very cheap on Aliexpress.

:::warning
Make sure to choose an **alloy steel** bolt rather than stainless steel, which is much weaker.
:::

To use a shoulder bolt, simply pass the bolt through the weapon and both weapon mounts, and secure it with a nylock nut.

![Shoulder bolt](/img/wiki/weapon-stacks/shoulder-bolt.png)

*A precision-ground shoulder bolt used as a cheap, simple weapon shaft.*

### Hardened Steel Shafting + Shaft Collars
Another option is to use a hardened steel shaft or rod of some sort, secured with **shaft collars**. This option allows you to use shafting made of materials that are more precise, harder, and stronger than a shoulder bolt, such as linear shaft, HSS tool blanks, or hardened dowel pins.

The downside of this option is that shaft collars are large and exposed, and are more likely to loosen than a nylock nut. Make sure to use **clamping shaft collars** as opposed to set-screw shaft collars, since they're much more secure.

![Shaft collars](/img/wiki/weapon-stacks/shaft-collar.png)

*Clamping shaft collars secure a hardened steel shaft in place.*

### End-Tapped Linear Shafts (Recommended)
The third option and most optimal solution is to use an **end-tapped linear shaft from Misumi**. This is a hardened steel shaft with tapped holes on either end, allowing it to be secured with screws. They are harder and stronger than a shoulder bolt, and do not suffer from exposed shaft collars. There is also a hollow option if you want to save some weight.

:::warning
If you do go with this option, make sure to pass the shaft itself through the weapon mount and constrain it with a washer under the screw, rather than simply bolting it to the inside of the weapon mounts. Additionally, use a strong threadlocker on the constraining screws to make sure they don't loosen.
:::

### Shaft Sizing Guidelines
- Hardened steel weapon shaft should be at least **5/16" or 8mm**. This thickness works well for smaller vertical spinners, but thicker shaft of around **3/8" or 10mm** is preferred for larger vertical spinners or wider ones like drums or beaters.
- Weapon shafts for horizontal spinners are generally thicker, often ranging from **3/8" to 1/2"**.
- **1/2" or 12mm hollow shaft** is also common for larger vertical spinners.
- Some people will choose to use Titanium shafts instead of steel to save weight, in which case they will need to be thicker. Titanium shaft is **not recommended** with needle roller bearings. Due to its lower hardness, the shaft will get dented by the hardened rollers in the bearing on hits.

## Power Transmission

The majority of robots will use either **gears** or **pulleys** to transfer power to the weapon to isolate shock from the weapon motor. While attaching a weapon directly to the motor is common and effective in smaller weight classes, it will not work in beetleweights unless the motor is custom-designed specifically for this purpose.

When using belts, most builders will attach a 3D-printed pulley to the side of their weapon. **TPU or Nylon** are good choices for printed pulleys. On the motor side, the most common solutions are to either attach an off-the-shelf aluminum pulley to the motor shaft, or to bolt a 3D-printed pulley to the screw holes on the back of an outrunner motor.

### Timing Belts & Pulleys
Timing belts and pulleys are the most common choice for weapon power transmission. The belts have teeth that engage with teeth on the pulleys to prevent slippage and reduce the need for accurate tensioning. There are many different standard tooth profiles, but the most commonly used in beetleweights are **S3M, HTD3M, HTD5M, and XL**.

To figure out how far apart your pulleys need to be for a given profile, belt size, and pulley size, you can use an online center-to-center distance calculator.

![Timing belt and pulley](/img/wiki/weapon-stacks/timing-belt-1.png)

*Timing belts and pulleys transfer power without slipping.*

**Downsides:** Because they have no slippage, shock can be transferred to the motor which can cause damage such as bent shafts. To mitigate this, some builders will choose to omit teeth on one or both of the pulleys and/or run the belt inverted to allow for slippage. One common method of doing this is by running a belt directly off the spinning can of an outrunner motor rather than using a pulley. Without teeth to engage, tensioning becomes important so it's recommended to use prototyping to find an optimal center-to-center distance, or implement some sort of tensioning mechanism.

![Slipping belt drive](/img/wiki/weapon-stacks/belt-slip.png)

*Letting the belt slip (for example by running it directly off the motor can) allows shock to be absorbed.*

### V-Belts
For a more robust option, **V-belts** are a good choice. Rather than teeth, v-belts have a v-shaped profile that increases friction with the pulleys to transfer torque more effectively while still allowing for some slip. Finding small v-belts can be challenging, but **Repeat Robotics** stocks some small sizes of 6mm cogged v-belt that are a great size for beetles.

One challenge with v-belts is that 3D-printing their pulleys is risky as the force of the belt's tension tends to split the pulley along its layer lines. Printed pulleys can work if printed in a strong material with good layer adhesion like unfilled nylon, but most builders who use v-belts use custom aluminum pulleys making them a more expensive option.

### Gears
Gears are a popular choice for weapon systems where the motor and weapon are fairly close together, such as undercutter or overhead horizontal spinners. They are simpler to design and more robust than pulley systems but less efficient, and since gears are usually 3D-printed, they can be prone to melting when subject to high RPMs.

One benefit of gears over timing belts is that because the teeth can be made very large, they are capable of transferring large amounts of torque and are much less sensitive to variations in center-to-center distance, making them ideal for lifter or hammer systems or systems where rigidity between components is poor. Most gears in combat robots are simple spur gears with a very large tooth module, and 3D-printed out of either TPU for large gears or nylon for smaller gears.

:::tip Designing gears & pulleys
Designing gears and timing pulleys manually in CAD is quite tedious, so it is recommended to use a pulley or gear generator. Some CAD software have these generators built in, but there are also several online options that will allow you to generate CAD files of gears or pulleys of different specifications.
:::

## Hubmotors

In the context of combat robotics, **hubmotors** refer to custom-built outrunner motors that are designed specifically to handle the shock loads of a weapon attached directly to the motor's spinning exterior. By eliminating the need for pulleys or gears, they can allow for a much more compact and robust weapon system.

![Hubmotor](/img/wiki/weapon-stacks/hubmotor.png)

*A hubmotor: a custom outrunner designed to handle weapon shock loads with the weapon mounted directly.*

Historically, the downside of a hubmotor was that it was very challenging and expensive to design and manufacture a motor capable of handling the shock loads experienced by the weapon system of a beetleweight. Most hubmotors used the stator and magnet ring from an off-the-shelf motor installed into a custom housing with greatly increased "beefyness" and much larger and better-positioned bearings.

Today, there are a few off-the-shelf options for beetleweight hubmotors that have made them much more popular. Hubmotors sold by **Repeat Robotics** and **Just 'Cuz Robotics** are easy to use, reliable, and come at a reasonable price. While they are still more expensive than a pulley or gear system with a regular motor, their ease of use and reliability makes them an excellent choice for beginners and experienced builders alike.

## Weapon Stacks

A **weapon stack** refers to the spinning assembly consisting of the weapon itself, pulleys/gears, bearings, and hardware, as well as its mounting to the rest of the robot. The durability of a weapon stack is crucial to the effectiveness of the weapon system as a whole.

### Vertical Spinner Weapon Stacks

Vertical spinner weapon stacks tend to be quite simple, as they tend to be smaller in diameter, reducing the amount of force exerted on the hub and shaft on hits.

**Single-Disk Vert Weapon Stacks** are very straightforward to build. Generally, bolting a 3D-printed pulley directly to the weapon and pressing one bearing in each side of the assembly (one in the weapon, and one in the pulley) will yield a sufficiently durable system. To isolate the weapon-side bearing from shocks, some builders may also choose to mount both bearings in the pulley/hub and use a keying feature to mount the weapon.

**Drum & Beater Weapon Stacks** can be built the same way as single-disk stacks, with a pulley bolted directly to the side of the weapon. Because these weapons tend to be wider, most builders choose to press one bearing into each side of the weapon rather than in the pulley. For a more durable setup however, some builders will choose to use aluminum inserts that hold the bearings and key into the drum or beater. The **Repeat Robotics Beater Assembly** is a good example of this.

**Drisk Weapon Stacks** (dual disks) can be tricky since if you hit with only one of the blades, all the inertia of the other one is transferred entirely through the hub causing a significant amount of twisting force. One popular method is to connect the two disks with large aluminum standoffs of M4 or larger, surrounded by a 3D-printed hub. The **Peter Bar Kit** weapon assembly is a good example of this. A more durable but expensive method is to design a custom aluminum hub that keys into both disks and contains the bearings.

### Horizontal Spinner Weapon Stacks

Weapon stack design is more crucial for horizontal spinners than for vertical spinners. Horizontal weapon stacks have to be very durable to handle the increased bending forces from a larger blade as well as more rigid to prevent the blade from deflecting into the robot's frame. As such, horizontal spinners tend to use more rigid materials and larger hardware in their hubs.

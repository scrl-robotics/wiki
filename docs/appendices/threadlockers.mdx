# Appendix III: Using Threadlockers

Needless to say, combat robots are exposed very frequently to strong shocks and vibrations. These can cause screws to shake loose, causing a plethora of issues. Thankfully, there are many products on the market that are designed specifically to solve this problem.

The most reliable way to avoid loosening is to use a **nylon locknut**. These nuts feature a nylon ring that tightly grips the screw and stops it from backing out. They are very cheap, hold tight, and are reusable a few times. However, many times you will be screwing into things other than a nut such as standoffs, motors, nutstrips, or tapped plates, so locknuts aren't always usable. This is where **threadlockers** come in.

Threadlockers are products designed to be applied to the threads of a screw. After the screw is installed, the threadlocker will cure and produce a tight hold that stops the screw from backing out due to vibration. It is recommended to always use threadlockers on any non-threadforming screws that aren't going into locknuts.

There are many different types of threadlockers for different purposes, and the below will be an overview of the most common ones.

## Blue Threadlockers

*(Loctite 242, 243, Permatex Blue, etc.)*

Blue threadlockers are the most standard **medium-strength** threadlocker. They hold tight but are still removable with hand tools. If you only want to use one type of threadlocker, this would be the one. It is a good choice for almost any combat robotics application.

## Red Threadlockers

*(Loctite 261, 262, 271, Permatex Red, etc.)*

Red threadlockers are a **high-strength** threadlocker. They are intended to be permanent, so have a much tighter hold than blue threadlockers. This makes them useful for:
- Parts that see very high vibrations such as weapon assemblies
- Parts that should never need to be disassembled such as gearboxes

Small screws such as M2 or M3 with red threadlockers are usually still removable, though you must be very careful and use a high-quality tool to avoid stripping the head. For larger screws, applying heat with a heat-gun or lighter will release the threadlocker.

## Orange Threadlockers

*(Loctite 266, Permatex Orange, etc.)*

Orange threadlockers lie somewhere between blue and red threadlockers in terms of strength. They are much less common though, and are often more expensive. They are also a good all-around choice.

## Vibra-Tite VC3

**Vibra-Tite VC3** is a very interesting product that is very powerful if used correctly. Unlike other threadlockers which are single-use and must be reapplied every time the screw is installed, this product is **re-usable**, meaning you don't have to reapply it and wait for it to cure every time you remove the screw.

It is expensive and difficult to find, but very useful for parts that need to be removed frequently.

## Curing and Primers

One of the downsides of threadlockers (and one of the main benefits of nylon locknuts, plastites, or VC3) is that they take a **long time to cure**. Most threadlockers begin to take effect after around an hour, but can take as long as **24 hours** to achieve full strength. This can make things difficult if your next fight is in 20 minutes.

Thankfully, there are some products that can rapidly increase the cure speed of threadlockers. Applied to the screw before applying threadlocker, **primers** such as Loctite 7649, Loctite 7471, Loctite 7088 Quickstix, or Vibra-Tite 611 can allow threadlockers to take effect in as little as **5 minutes** and cure fully in an hour. Picking up one of these products is highly recommended as they can be a life-saver at competitions.

:::warning
When using primers, be careful not to get any on the tip of your threadlocker bottle. This will cause the threadlocker to cure and clog the bottle. They are also generally toxic and have a strong scent, so be careful when using them indoors.

A popular tip is to pour them into glass brush-top nail polish bottles and use the brush to apply them to screws.
:::
